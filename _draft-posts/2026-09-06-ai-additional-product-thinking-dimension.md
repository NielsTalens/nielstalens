## An unique technology hype:

The tech industry is characterized by endless hype cycles.
Every couple of years there is a new promise. 
Often one of enormous increase of speed or infinite scalability.
Or, and this is the one that most frequently reappears, a way to no longer need software developers.

Most of these hypes focus on one part of technology.
AI differs because it can touch almost every part of a business at the same time.
It touches:
- Technology.
- Ways of working.
- Economics.
- Compliance.
- Security.
- Data.
- Customer interaction.
- Product development.

??? We use it to build products or use it as part of the product.
Or both.
Use it to interact with customers and to operate the product.

## The push

It started with a very targeted push what was followed by a very polarized type of discussing.
A lot of FOMO building.

We have seen it before. 
There is a new technology like block chain.
The only way for it to survive it make sure it is used as much as possible.
So it is presented as the solution for all problems.
And companies specialized in the technology are popping up.


The bottom line now is that there are two major AI companies today, who lose X per year, and are the sole customers of data centers.
And the companies that build and own these data centers are the main investors in these AI companies. 
Circulair investing.
Once every given period one of these companies claims something spectacular.
It is needed to keep the hype going.
And that doesn't need to be a problem. They are just companies. If they cease to exist other models will reappear.

## Compliance is very different this time

Q: what are you main concerns?

Traditionally compliance mostly constrained the result but while using AI, either to build the product or use in the product, it becomes part of the production system itself.
Compliance is no longer only about whether the outcome is acceptable. 
It increasingly becomes part of the architecture of how the outcome is produced.
With traditional software, you could often validate the system and then deploy it.
With AI, the behaviour of the system itself becomes something that may need continuous governance.
You will have to have answers for a wider scope of questions like:
- What are we building?
- How was it built?
- What data went into it?
- How was that data obtained and governed?
- How did the system arrive at this outcome?
- Can we explain or reproduce it?
- Who is accountable when it is wrong?
- How are employees allowed to use it?
- How do customers interact with it?
- What happens when the model changes?

## Different perspectives and use cases under one name

Where other technical hypes often touch one subject (like speed of development or scalability) or a new way to create things (low code) in the case of AI, it touches many subjects. 
Also many use case perspectives but also actors.
We have marketeers who can us it to do research and sales who use it to create proposals.
We have engineers who use it to create code and marketeers who use it to create campaigns.
We now all have that handy colleague who creates all kinds of agents and security who uses it for planning their day or compose emails.
So all these actors have a different perspective when we talk about AI.

The question 'can we use AI' is strongly depending on the context.

Q: do you see this wide scope as compliance people?

## Product management 

In regards to product management we often have three main questions:
- Can we do it? — technology
- May we do it? — compliance
- Should we do it? — customer and business value

The first two are dominating the discussion about AI (or any new technology in that sense).
The third one is about value.

The main issue regarding these questions in product development is often the sequence in which they are asked.
Being able to do something from a technical and compliance perspective does often not mean you should do it.
Remember block chain?

Can we replace a claims conversation with an AI interaction? Probably.
May we? Possibly, with the right controls.
Should we?
That is a completely different question.

Does it improve trust? Does it reduce friction? Does it remove something customers actually valued? Does the organisation save €2 while creating €20 of downstream frustration?
Are we just automating because we can or are we automating because it serves our customers?
